# Escalation Tiers

On most support teams issues are handled by tier. A tier is a level, generally from 1 to 3, designed to make sure that support issues are handled in a timely and organized manner. A new or seasoned support agent shouldn't be offended by the tier they're placed on. Even if they bring experience from another company, a Support Manager or Team Lead may start them on Tier One as a new hire simply to learn the product and get acquainted with the issues clients most commonly report. It's also one of the best ways to build rapport with customers before moving deeper into the support process.

Before explaining the tiers, two terms worth understanding are B2B and B2C.

---

## B2B vs. B2C

---

### B2B: Business to Business

B2B means the product or service provider is a business and the client is also a business. When searching for support roles you will often see the term "enterprise level" in job descriptions. This generally means the company is looking for someone with experience supporting larger organizations as clients, and by extension someone who is comfortable working with directors, administrators, and C-level executive leadership when resolving issues.

---

### B2C: Business to Consumer

B2C means the business is the provider but the customer is an individual consumer rather than another business. Some support organizations use the word "client" for both models but the term is more accurately associated with B2B. A good B2C example is a music streaming service like Apple Music or Spotify. Their customers are individual subscribers. When a subscriber has a technical issue they contact support as one person, not as a representative of an organization. Another example is a smartphone owner calling the manufacturer directly about a hardware issue. The business is Samsung. The customer is the individual phone owner.

---

## Tier One

Tier One, sometimes called Level One, is in many ways the most important tier on a support team. It is the frontline. Tier One agents are the first point of contact via phone, email, or chat and they handle a high volume of issues ranging from the simple and repetitive to the beginning of a deeper investigation.

Simple Tier One resolutions include things like password resets, reboots, restarting a service, or walking a customer through something they have forgotten how to do. These are fast to resolve and require good product knowledge and a calm, confident demeanor.

For issues that won't be resolved quickly, the Tier One agent serves as the first line of investigation. If the issue cannot be resolved within approximately one hour, it gets escalated so the agent can move through their queue and remain available for incoming cases. Part of that judgment call involves priority. The Tier One agent determines which unresolved issues carry the most business impact and should be escalated first.

Tier One agents also have to have exceptional bedside manner. Customers are generally at their most frustrated when they first report an issue. An agent who can absorb urgency, respond with accountability, and immediately reassure the customer that they are all over it, while actually being all over it, is the standard. The Tier One agent treats the customer's issue like it is their own problem to solve.

Tier One agents are also often the first to identify an outage. When multiple customers report the same issue in a short window, the Tier One team is positioned to notice the trend and escalate it to support leadership before it becomes a larger problem.

When an issue cannot be resolved at Tier One, the agent documents their steps, informs the customer that the issue is being escalated, and follows the escalation process established by support leadership before moving on.

---

## Tier Two

Tier Two carries a wide range of responsibilities. Primarily they are the escalation point for issues Tier One was unable to resolve. Depending on the priority of what lands on their desk, a Tier Two agent may need to set aside their current work to address something of higher urgency that just came in.

When picking up an escalated case, the Tier Two agent reviews the Tier One notes and will typically attempt to reproduce the steps taken before adding their own investigation. If something in the notes is unclear they may contact the Tier One agent directly to fill in the gaps. The Tier Two team is generally smaller than Tier One and may assist Tier One during particularly busy periods or when staffing is short.

Customer communication at Tier Two often requires more expectation setting. When an investigation is going to take time the Tier Two agent has to explain the situation clearly without getting the customer too deep into the technical. This means translating what one system has to do with another in plain language and managing frustration with patience and calm. The customer will not always be happy about timelines. The Tier Two agent has to be more composed than the customer in those moments.

Tier Two agents are also educators and informal leaders. They mentor Tier One agents on note taking, basic knowledge base writing, and issue awareness. If a Tier Two agent finds a solution that Tier One could handle in the future, they write it up and share it with the team regardless of tier so everyone benefits. They serve as a sounding board for agents who are frustrated with a customer or a product limitation and can provide grounded, constructive feedback.

Knowledge base writing, both internal and external, is a core Tier Two responsibility. A well written knowledge base speeds up resolution at every tier and gives customers the option to self serve when they prefer to.

When a Tier Two investigation reaches its time limit without resolution, the issue escalates to Tier Three.

---

## Tier Three

Tier Three is generally held by a Team Lead or a designated Team Lead backup. They are the escalation point for Tier Two and have the longest allowable investigation window of any tier. By the time an issue reaches Tier Three it is typically complex, persistent, and serious.

The Tier Three agent reviews the steps taken at the previous two tiers and may already have a direction in mind based on what they see in the notes. Unlike the lower tiers, Tier Three is not just recreating steps. They spend significant time attempting to break things the same way the customer broke them, using a test environment to reproduce the conditions that caused the issue. They take detailed notes throughout because those notes may need to be handed to an engineer or product manager in a clear, readable format. Detailed but not so long that nobody reads them.

Tier Three is the ultimate collaborator on a support team. They work across Product, Engineering, Customer Success, Sales, and Executive Leadership when necessary. What they share varies by audience. A Customer Success manager may only need a general summary while an Engineering team may need raw logs reviewed line by line. Tier Three also checks past versions, old documentation, similar historical cases, databases, configurations, settings, application logs, and error logs as part of their investigation.

Throughout all of this, the Tier Three agent keeps the customer updated and continues to manage expectations just as the lower tiers do. They prioritize based on business impact and communicate what the next steps look like without creating panic or over-promising timelines.

Tier Three agents are also mentors to both previous tiers and are responsible for writing internal documentation, particularly around what was discovered in collaboration with Engineering or Product, so that if the same issue surfaces again, it does not have to travel through all three tiers before it is recognized and resolved.

---

## Last Lines of Escalation

When Tier Three has done everything within the scope of support and the fix requires a product or engineering team to intervene, the ticket ownership shifts. Engineering or Product takes the issue in hand while the Tier Three agent retains the customer relationship and continues providing updates. This is an important distinction.

Engineers should generally not be communicating directly with the customer. That is the job of support, up to and including the Support Manager. Engineering provides their findings, ETAs, and status updates back to the support team who then translates that into customer communication. This protects the customer experience and keeps the technical conversation where it belongs.

---

## Conclusion

No matter the company, support teams can and do get buried in tickets. While everyone on a support team should carry a leadership mindset, it is support leadership that keeps an eye on the overall team across all tiers and manages coverage when someone is out or volume spikes unexpectedly.

The escalation process is only as effective as the people who execute it. Leadership sets the tone. A team with strong leadership moves issues to the right people quickly and manages them well. A team without it stalls.

On a personal note I have worked on teams that handled escalation differently. Once by time zone and once by assigned accounts where one agent carried a case no matter how complex it became. Both approaches frustrated customers significantly. Time zone routing created delays. Assigned account models created bottlenecks. A good support team uses a system that works for its customers first. Whatever that system looks like, it needs to move the right issue to the right person as fast as possible.
